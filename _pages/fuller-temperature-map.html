A World Map for a Game

A fun side project, and a quick guide to one of the strangest-looking world maps there is.

Did you know Buckminster Fuller taught at SIU?

Before he was famous for the geodesic dome, R. Buckminster Fuller spent twelve years in Carbondale. From 1959 to 1971 he was a research professor at Southern Illinois University, and from 1960 he and his wife Anne lived in a geodesic dome at 407 South Forest Avenue, a few blocks from campus. It was the only dome he ever lived in and the only home he ever owned. It still stands, and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Fuller Dome Home →

His idea: a world map with no "up"

Fuller also rethought the world map. Every flat map has to cut and stretch the globe somewhere. Most familiar maps cut the oceans and stretch the land near the poles, and they put north at the top by convention.

Fuller's answer, the Dymaxion map, wraps the globe in an icosahedron, a solid with 20 triangular faces, and unfolds those triangles flat. He first published a version in Life magazine in 1943, and the icosahedral version followed in 1954, developed with the cartographer Shoji Sadao. The cuts are placed so the continents stay almost joined, like one island in one ocean. There is no right-side-up: Fuller wanted people to see the Earth as a single connected place, not as nations arranged around a north-up grid. Buckminster Fuller Institute →

Every projection makes trade-offs, and this one does too. The Fuller projection is neither equal-area nor conformal, so areas, shapes, distances and directions are all somewhat distorted, and lines of latitude and longitude break at the triangle edges. In return, distortion is spread fairly evenly and no continent gets cut apart. It's a map for seeing the whole planet, not for measuring distances. Esri: Fuller projection →

Bringing the map back to Carbondale

In 2026, the Fuller Futures Festival in Carbondale planned a large-format version of Fuller's map for a public game. Dr. Justin Schoof (SIUC Geography and Environmental Resources) supplied global temperature data, and I built the map in ArcGIS, putting land and sea-surface temperatures onto Fuller's triangles, with legends, coastlines and labels so players could find their way around.

It was a fun one to make: a geographer at Fuller's old university, drawing his map of the world more than eighty years after he first published it, for a festival that carries his name.

How to read this map

  • The triangles are the unfolded faces of the icosahedron. The white lines are the folds. Coastlines, labels and the latitude/longitude grid help you find familiar places in the unfamiliar arrangement.
  • Two legends, two temperature scales. Land uses the green-to-red scale at the top, from below −40 °C to above 30 °C. Oceans use the blue sea-surface-temperature scale at the bottom. Read each color against its own legend.
  • Try finding Antarctica (purple, coldest), then follow the continents around: they nearly touch all the way across.
