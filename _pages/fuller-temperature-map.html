A World Map for a Game

A fun side project, and a quick guide to one of the strangest-looking world maps there is.

A map to gather around

Most world maps are rectangles. This one is made of triangles. I built it in ArcGIS from temperature data supplied by Dr. Justin Schoof (SIUC Geography), for a large-format game at the Fuller Futures Festival in Carbondale, home of Buckminster Fuller's dome house.

It was a good excuse to get to know a projection most people have never seen. Here's how to read it, and why it looks the way it does.

How to read the map

The white triangular edges show the facets of the unfolded globe. Coastlines, geographic labels and graticules provide orientation across the unfamiliar arrangement of continents.

The upper legend describes the land-temperature classes in degrees Celsius, from below −40 °C to above 30 °C. The lower legend describes sea-surface-temperature classes in degrees Celsius with a separate blue palette. These are two separate legends: land and ocean colors should be interpreted using their corresponding scales.

What is the Fuller projection?

The Fuller projection, also called the Dymaxion map, represents the globe using the 20 triangular faces of an icosahedron. These faces are unfolded into a flat arrangement designed to avoid splitting continental landmasses. The familiar 1954 map was developed by R. Buckminster Fuller with cartographer Shoji Sadao. Buckminster Fuller Institute →

Its connected-land view supports thinking about global relationships rather than treating each continent as a separate island in a rectangular world map. It also asks the reader to use coastlines, labels and graticules instead of relying on a familiar north-up layout.

A projection still makes tradeoffs. Fuller is neither equal-area nor conformal. Areas, shapes, distances, directions and angles can be distorted, and geographic lines are interrupted at facet boundaries. It is intended for whole-globe representation, rather than local distance measurement. Esri’s projection documentation →

Fuller’s Carbondale connection

The Fuller Dome Home in Carbondale provides the local connection between this mapping project and Fuller’s legacy. The festival’s planned use of the map brings cartography into that public setting. Fuller Dome Home →
